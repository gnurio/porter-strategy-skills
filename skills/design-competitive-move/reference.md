# Reference: design-competitive-move

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Even with clear strengths, a war of attrition is costly to victor and vanquished alike -- avoid it.
- The more the competitor thinks you border on irrational in pursuing your commitment, the more wary they will be.
- Behavior communicated to suppliers, customers, channels, and the public all contribute to perceived seriousness.
- Buyers may report secret price cuts that never occurred to encourage discounting -- verify before retaliating.
- If retaliation must be generalized rather than focused on the initiator, discipline becomes risky.
- **Competitive moves are a game of finesse, not just brute force.** The goal is a move "whose outcome is quickly determined (no protracted or serious battle takes place) and also skewed as much as possible toward the firm's own interests" -- ideally, a battle of retaliation never begins at all.
- **Asymmetric cost is a lever:** if a firm can find moves that are much less costly for it to make than for competitors to answer, it produces retaliation lag or deters response altogether (the mirror image of the failure mode where a large firm must generalize its own response at "enormous expense").
- **Denying the base can be worth paying a short-run price for.** If a firm's market position is threatened, tactics like special deals to load customers with inventory, or attacking a competitor's new product while still in test-market (cheaper than waiting for full launch), can convince the entrant it will never hit its growth/ROI targets and cause it to withdraw.
- **Focal points reduce costly jockeying.** Identify a desirable "resting place" for competitive expectations (round-number market share, standard price point, informal geographic split) as early as possible -- the faster reached, the lower the cost of jockeying. You can also shape which focal point emerges, e.g. by introducing terminology ("price per square foot") that favors your position.
- **Irreversibility is what makes a commitment credible.** A publicized long-term contract, buying (not leasing) a plant, full integration instead of assembly-only entry, and public targets all make backing down costly and embarrassing -- which is exactly what gives the commitment deterrent value.
- **Detection capability is essential to a credible retaliation threat.** If a competitor believes it can cheat (shade prices, adjust quality) undetected, it will. Demonstrable monitoring of sales, customers, and distributors makes the retaliation threat credible.
- **Trust is itself a form of commitment.** Demonstrably taking some diminution in performance that benefits competitors (e.g., yielding share in a downturn to avoid a price war, later reclaiming it in the upturn) is a persuasive way to de-escalate and build cooperative expectations.
- **Selective disclosure is a strategic weapon.** Detailed disclosure of an impending product lets competitors focus their response precisely; vague disclosure forces them to prepare for a range of possibilities, which is more costly for them. Treat information release as a deliberate part of the move, not an operational afterthought.
- **A slow-but-tough retaliation can leave the initiator worse off than a fast-but-weaker one** -- do not treat "maximize the lag" as an unqualified principle; balance lag against retaliation effectiveness.

## Failure Modes

1. **Brute force without finesse** -- treating competition as purely a resource game; even superior resources fail against desperate or irrational competitors.
2. **Hollow commitment** -- making threats without visible assets, track record, or irreversibility; competitors call the bluff.
3. **Ignoring mixed motives** -- attacking head-on when a flanking move would paralyze the competitor's response.
4. **Generalized retaliation** -- retaliating broadly instead of targeting the aggressor; triggers chain reaction across the industry.
5. **No deny-the-base hypothesis** -- defending without understanding the attacker's performance targets and time horizon.
6. **Misreading cooperative moves** -- treating a nonthreatening move as an attack and retaliating, destroying value for all.
7. **Treating "delay retaliation" as an unqualified principle** -- a slow-but-tough response can leave you worse off than a quick, less-effective one; the lag/effectiveness tradeoff must be balanced case by case.
8. **Careless information disclosure** -- releasing detailed plans that make it easy for competitors to prepare a precise defense, when vagueness would have forced them to hedge across multiple scenarios.
