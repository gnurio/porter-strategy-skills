# Reference: select-generic-strategy

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Cost leadership demands a firm be THE cost leader, not one of several. Failing to achieve this leaves the firm "in an extremely poor strategic situation."
- Differentiation does not mean ignoring costs -- but cost is not the primary strategic target.
- Focus works by serving a narrow target "more effectively or efficiently" than broad competitors. It achieves either low cost or differentiation (or both) within its segment.
- Each strategy requires "total commitment." Half-measures produce stuck-in-the-middle outcomes.
- Different strategies "require different styles of leadership and can translate into very different corporate cultures." Different sorts of people will be attracted.
- **A low-cost position defends against all five forces simultaneously**: it withstands rivalry (competitors compete away profits before the cost leader's margin), buyer power (buyers can only bargain down to the next-most-efficient competitor's costs), supplier power (more flexibility to absorb input cost increases), entry (the same factors that create low cost usually create entry barriers), and substitutes (favorable relative position vs. competitors facing the same substitute).
- **Cost leadership can revolutionize an industry** where historical competition was on other bases and rivals are perceptually or economically unprepared for cost-based attack.
- **Achieving differentiation may preclude high market share** -- it often requires a perception of exclusivity that is incompatible with broad-market volume (though not always: Caterpillar achieved both).
- **The focus strategy always trades profitability against sales volume** -- narrow targeting inherently caps achievable share.
- **Once "stuck in the middle," firms tend to flip back and forth** among the three generic strategies over time. Given the inconsistencies this creates, that flip-flopping approach is "almost always doomed to failure" -- committing to one strategy and enduring the transition is more reliable than oscillating.

## Failure Modes

1. **Attempting cost leadership without scale or capital access** -- leads to cost disadvantage, not advantage.
2. **Differentiating on attributes buyers do not value** -- pays the cost of differentiation without the premium.
3. **Focusing on a segment too similar to the broader market** -- broad competitors serve it equally well.
4. **Hedging across strategies** -- "conflicting organizational arrangements and motivation systems" guarantee mediocrity.
5. **Ignoring risks** -- e.g., a cost leader blind to product/marketing shifts (Ford in the 1920s, whose cost-minimization rigidities left it unable to respond as buyers began valuing styling and model variety over price).
6. **Confusing operational effectiveness with strategy** -- efficiency improvements available to all competitors are not a generic strategy.
7. **Risk of cost leadership going stale:** technological change nullifying past investment/learning, low-cost imitation by newcomers with state-of-the-art facilities, inflation narrowing the price gap that funded the cost advantage.
8. **Risk of differentiation eroding:** the cost gap to low-cost rivals growing too large for brand loyalty to hold; buyers' need for the differentiator falling as they grow more sophisticated; imitation narrowing perceived uniqueness as the industry matures.
9. **Risk of focus failing:** the cost gap between broad-line and focused competitors widening enough to erase the focuser's advantage; the target segment's needs converging with the broad market's; or a rival "outfocusing the focuser" by finding a sub-segment within the target.
