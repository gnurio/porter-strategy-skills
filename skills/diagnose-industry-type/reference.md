# Reference: diagnose-industry-type

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Industry maturity does not occur at any fixed point in an industry's development -- it can be delayed by innovations or other events that fuel continued growth.
- Mature industries may regain rapid growth through strategic breakthroughs and go through more than one transition to maturity.
- Fragmented structure can be a feature, not a bug -- some industries have fundamental economic reasons for staying fragmented (diseconomies of scale, diverse market needs).
- In emerging industries, the absence of rules is both a risk and a source of opportunity.
- Declining industries can still be profitable if exit barriers are low and remaining demand pockets have favorable characteristics.
- **The product life cycle is the "grandfather" of evolution concepts but is deeply flawed as a diagnostic tool**: stage duration varies widely, growth doesn't always follow the S-curve (some industries skip maturity or revitalize after decline), and the nature of competition at each stage differs radically by industry. Use the 14 evolutionary processes, not the life cycle, as the real diagnostic engine.
- **Every industry begins with an initial structure "usually (though not always) a far cry from the configuration the industry will take later."** Don't assume current structure predicts future structure.
- **Industries evolve as an interrelated system, not piecemeal** -- change in one structural element (e.g., a marketing innovation opening a new buyer segment) tends to trigger a chain reaction of changes elsewhere (e.g., manufacturing scale economies, backward integration).
- **Buyers become commodity-minded through repeat purchasing** -- products naturally trend toward being commodities over time as buyers get more sophisticated and better-informed. This is a structural signal of transition to maturity, not just a symptom.
- **Proprietary knowledge diffuses inevitably**: through physical inspection of competitors' products, embodiment in capital goods bought from outside suppliers, and personnel turnover (spin-offs, poaching). Treat "how proprietary is this technology right now" as a decaying, not static, signal.
- **The maturity cost/service shift requires a "way of life" change**, not just tactics -- slower growth and more knowledgeable buyers demand heightened "financial consciousness," which can be organizationally difficult for firms whose culture formed during the growth phase.
- **Company distress during transition to maturity creates a buy-cheap-assets opportunity** -- assets can often be acquired well below replacement cost during the shakeout.
- **A firm that is not the overall cost leader in a mature market can still find a different cost curve** that makes it the lowest-cost producer for a specific buyer type, product variety, or order size -- the structural basis for a focus strategy in maturity.

## Failure Modes

1. **Confusing cyclical decline with structural decline.** Apply the structural vs. cyclical test before classifying as declining.
2. **Ignoring the fragmented overlay.** An industry can be both mature AND fragmented -- don't force a single label.
3. **Premature maturity diagnosis.** A growth slowdown may be temporary; look for innovation breakthroughs that could restart growth.
4. **Insufficient data.** Don't classify with high confidence on 1-2 signals. State what data is missing and what would change the diagnosis.
5. **Ignoring adjacent industry shifts.** Evolutionary process #12 (structural change in adjacent industries) is frequently overlooked but can reshape classification.
6. **Treating the life cycle as a self-fulfilling prophecy.** Companies can actively affect the shape of the growth curve through product innovation and repositioning; taking the life cycle as an inevitable given becomes an undesirable self-fulfilling prophecy.
7. **Greeting evolution as a fait accompli to be reacted to, rather than an opportunity** to actively shape the industry's rules of the game.
8. **The cash trap.** Investing fresh cash to build share in a mature, slow-growing market usually requires heroic assumptions to justify -- maturity works against recouping the investment.
9. **Irrational resistance to price competition or changed industry practices** ("we will not compete on price," "they are hurting the industry") -- a common but dangerous reaction during transition to maturity that costs the resisting firm share.
10. **Overemphasis on "creative, new" products instead of aggressively selling and standardizing existing ones** as maturity sets in -- innovative focus should shift to standardization, not stay fixed on novelty.
11. **Clinging to "higher quality" as an excuse for not matching aggressive pricing** -- quality differentials erode as industries mature, and more knowledgeable buyers will trade unneeded quality for lower price.
12. **Giving up market share too easily for short-run profit** during transition to maturity, damaging future position where scale economies are becoming more significant.
