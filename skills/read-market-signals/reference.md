# Reference: read-market-signals

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Every signal can be a bluff. Never treat any single signal as definitive without corroboration.
- The less damaging the chosen action relative to what was feasible, the more likely it is conciliatory.
- Fighting brands introduced with little push or support before any serious attack are warnings; those backed with full resources are offensive weapons. A fighting brand that copies a specific competitor's product is more effective discipline than a generalized new product.
- Private antitrust suits are the mildest signal of displeasure -- since the suit can be dropped at any time by the initiator, treat it as a cross-parry equivalent, potentially harassment or a delaying tactic rather than genuine escalation.
- Too much attention to signals can itself be a distraction. Focus analysis time on signals that would materially change your strategic choices -- but note that ignoring signals altogether "is like ignoring competitors altogether."
- **Formality is a commitment signal:** the more formal an announcement (official press release vs. trade-journal mention), the more the announcing firm wants the message heard broadly, and the more it establishes a "public" commitment that is hard to walk back.
- **The audience shapes the meaning.** An announcement in a specialized trade journal is likely noticed only by industry participants -- a different connotation than a statement to securities analysts or the national business press.
- **Divergence from industry precedent is usually aggressive** -- e.g., discounting a product line that has never been discounted, or building a plant in an entirely new geography.
- **Cross-parry severity depends on target market:** a parry into a competitor's "bread and butter" market is a serious warning; a parry into a minor market is a milder warning that hopes to avoid triggering hasty escalation while signaling more is coming if the initiator doesn't back off.
- **Divergent market shares amplify a cross-parry's leverage** -- if the cross-parrying firm has small share in the contested market, matching its price cut costs the larger-share initiator disproportionately more.
- **Maintaining a small position in cross-markets is itself a useful deterrent**, independent of any active conflict.
- **Study history to read signals accurately.** Look for a firm's recurring behavioral sequences (does a sales-force push always precede a product change? does capacity get announced at a consistent utilization threshold?) -- these reveal unconscious, firm-specific signaling patterns.
- **Public commentary on industry conditions is self-interested.** A firm describing industry conditions (e.g., "prices are too high") may be trying to steer competitors toward an outcome that benefits itself -- verify independently rather than accepting the framing.
- **Ex post announcements can mislead**, e.g. quoting a new plant's eventual full capacity as if it were initial capacity, or including adjacent-product sales to inflate apparent market share.
- **A seemingly conciliatory announcement can be a double-edged sword** -- designed to lull competitors into complacency ahead of an aggressive move, not just to avoid one.

## Failure Modes

- **Mirror-imaging:** assuming the competitor thinks like you. Always ground interpretation in their goals and assumptions, not yours.
- **Over-reading a single signal:** one data point is not a pattern. Corroborate with historical behavior and multiple signal types.
- **Ignoring the medium:** treating a narrow-audience trial balloon the same as a formal public commitment.
- **Neglecting divergence risk:** assuming historical patterns will always hold. Changed economics or leadership can break patterns.
- **Confusing conciliation with weakness:** a sub-optimal move may be an invitation to cooperate, not a sign the competitor cannot do more.
- **Taking public commentary at face value** without verifying independently -- the speaker may be shaping perception to its own advantage.
- **Trusting ex post announcements uncritically**, particularly unaudited figures like market share or newly announced capacity.
- **Treating signal-monitoring as a pure distraction** and disengaging from it entirely -- Porter is explicit that this is equivalent to ignoring competitors altogether.
