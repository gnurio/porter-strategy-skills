# Reference: strategize-declining-industry

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Selling assets early recovers far more value than selling after decline is universally recognized.
- Assets sold within the industry (not retired) can worsen competition for remaining firms if the buyer operates on a lower cost basis -- selling a plant to entrepreneurs or employees at a discount to book value is "even worse for subsequent competition" than if the original owner had stayed, because the buyer's lower investment base lets it make pricing decisions that cripple remaining firms.
- The strengths that matter in decline are NOT necessarily the strengths that mattered during growth -- they relate to the remaining pockets of demand, which can favor entirely different capabilities.
- Harvest fails in volatile industries: competitors immediately seize on lack of investment to grab share or bid down prices.
- Government subsidies keeping ailing firms alive are nearly as bad as high exit barriers -- capacity stays in the market, and the subsidized firm distorts pricing by deciding on non-market economics.
- **If liquidation value is low relative to book value, staying can be the economically correct choice** even while reporting a book loss -- the discounted future cash flows may still exceed the opportunity cost of the capital that would be freed by exiting.
- **The worst rivalry scenario in decline:** one or two weak firms with real corporate-level resources and a strong strategic commitment to stay, forced into desperate action (price cuts) that threatens the whole industry's economics.
- **The leadership premise:** achieving leadership puts the firm in a superior position to either hold or harvest later than it otherwise could. Tactics include buying competitors' share/capacity above their outside sale value specifically to *lower their exit barriers* and guarantee the capacity is retired rather than resold within the industry.
- **Harvest's prerequisite is genuine past strength.** Without real strengths to draw on, a firm is limited to invisible harvesting actions (deferred maintenance, dropping marginal accounts) rather than visible ones (price increases, service cuts) -- visible actions without underlying strength simply drive customers away.
- **Harvest is the most administratively demanding decline strategy**, even though this is rarely discussed in the literature -- a controlled liquidation wreaks havoc on employee morale, retention, and supplier/customer confidence, and provides poor motivation for the executives asked to run a "dog."
- **Early divestment trades certainty for value.** Divesting before decline is obvious maximizes resale value (buyers haven't repriced for decline yet), but risks that the pessimistic forecast turns out wrong.
- **Postponing the decline-strategy choice eliminates the polar options.** An early commitment to Leadership signals incumbents to exit and buys timing advantage; an early commitment to Divest captures value. Delaying the decision tends to foreclose both, pushing the firm toward Niche or Harvest by default rather than by choice.
- **The most objective firms about decline are usually the ones that also participate in the substitute industry** -- they have a clearer, less identity-clouded view of the substitution threat than pure incumbents do.

## Failure Modes

1. **Harvesting in a volatile industry** -- Competitors immediately steal share when you cut investment; no cash flow benefit materializes.
2. **Pursuing Leadership without credibility** -- Competitors do not believe you will outlast them; investment is wasted in prolonged warfare.
3. **Hanging on due to emotional attachment** -- Managerial pride prevents rational exit; the firm bleeds cash in an inhospitable industry.
4. **Misreading remaining demand** -- Assuming remaining pockets are price-insensitive when they are actually commodity-like and threatened by further substitutes.
5. **Divesting too late** -- Waiting until decline is obvious destroys liquidation value; buyers have all the bargaining power.
6. **Ignoring competitor exit barriers** -- Failing to predict which competitors are trapped leads to underestimating rivalry intensity.
7. **A war of attrition against high-exit-barrier competitors** -- they are forced to respond vigorously and will not yield position without significant investment; this "usually leads to disaster" for the initiator.
8. **Failure to recognize decline at all** -- long identification with the industry, or an overly narrow view of what counts as a substitute, causes firms to stay optimistic well past the point of objectivity. High exit barriers compound this: managers look for optimistic signs because pessimistic ones are painful to act on.
