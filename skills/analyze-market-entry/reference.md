# Reference: analyze-market-entry

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Financial analyses commonly undercount entry costs by ignoring intangible barriers and retaliation.
- The most common error is measuring only visible investments and assuming pre-entry prices will persist.
- Joint ventures should be analyzed using the same framework as internal entry.
- A sequenced strategy often lowers risk because the firm can fail cheap before committing to irreversible investments.
- In the market for companies, large premiums over market value are the rule, not the exception.
- **If market forces work perfectly, no entry decision can yield above-average returns** -- expected entrant profits should just reflect the height of structural barriers and the legitimate expectation of retaliation. Above-average returns require finding a market that is NOT working perfectly: an industry in disequilibrium, an area where retaliation will be slow, or a situation where the firm has a distinctive ability to overcome barriers cheaply.
- **New entry itself raises input costs** -- scarce supplies, equipment, or labor get bid up, meaning the entrant bears inherently higher costs than incumbents did.
- **Retaliation is most volatile** in industries with slow growth, commodity-like products, high fixed costs, high concentration, and incumbents who attach high strategic importance to the business.
- **The "sleeping giant" opportunity:** a paternal dominant incumbent that sees itself as the industry's protector may be slow to learn and respond, letting an entrant build a real position -- but the risk is that the giant wakes up, so judge its management character carefully before relying on this.
- **Convincing incumbents you will never give up can itself deter retaliation** -- they may conclude it isn't worth the money to try to dislodge you.
- **In acquisitions, the ability to improve the target is not sufficient on its own** -- the ability must be *distinctive*, or other bidders will see the same potential and bid the price up until the improvement's value is competed away.
- **Sequenced entry lowers both cost and risk**: it accumulates knowledge and brand identification usable at no extra cost for the next move, develops managerial talent gradually, tempers incumbent reaction (a smaller initial move looks less threatening), segments risk (failure at the first step is cheap), and lets the firm start with reversible investments before committing to irreversible ones.
- **A strong position in one stage does not automatically extend to an adjacent one.** Forward or backward integration into a more competitive business can destroy value even when the base business is strong -- e.g., a strong manufacturer integrating into competitive retailing might be better served by many retailers competing to sell its product.
- **Experience in one part of a vertical chain doesn't qualify management to run another.** Applying historical managerial approaches to a very different upstream or downstream business is a documented way to destroy the new unit.
- **Firms enter emerging industries too often just because they are growing fast or currently profitable.** The decision must rest on a structural analysis of the industry's ultimate long-run structure, not its current trajectory.

## Failure Modes

- **Counting only visible costs**: ignoring brand franchise, channel access, proprietary technology, and inflated input prices.
- **Assuming pre-entry prices hold**: failing to adjust pro forma for retaliation-driven price depression, which commonly persists for years after entry.
- **Ignoring retaliation probability**: treating entry as if incumbents will not respond.
- **Overpaying for acquisitions**: assuming you can improve the target when your ability is not distinctive (other bidders see the same potential).
- **Skipping sequenced entry analysis**: going straight for the ultimate target group when a lower-risk stepping-stone exists.
- **Mistaking a bidder's willingness to raise price for evidence of value**: the bidder may have non-economic motives (growth objectives, management idiosyncrasies).
- **The adjacency illusion**: assuming a strong position in one stage of the value chain automatically transfers to an adjacent, more competitive stage.
- **The management transfer trap**: assuming operating experience in one business qualifies leadership to run a structurally different one.
- **Entering because an industry is growing fast, not because structural analysis supports it.**
