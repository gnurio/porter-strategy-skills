# Reference: analyze-five-forces

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- Even a firm with a strong market position earns low returns if it faces a superior, lower-cost substitute.
- An industry with blocked entry and no substitutes can still have poor profitability if rivalry is intense. Even with no substitutes and blocked entry, intense rivalry among existing competitors limits potential returns.
- The extreme case of competitive intensity is the economist's perfectly competitive industry: entry is free, existing firms have no bargaining power against suppliers and customers, and rivalry is unbridled because the numerous firms and products are all alike.
- Forces outside the industry (macroeconomic, societal) usually affect all firms equally -- their true strategic significance lies in the differing abilities of firms to deal with them, not in the forces themselves.
- Acquisition into an industry with intent to build market position should be viewed as entry, even though no entirely new entity is created.
- **Entry/exit barrier matrix:** Best case for industry profits is high entry barriers + low exit barriers (entry deterred, failures leave cleanly). Worst case is low entry barriers + high exit barriers (capacity stacks up, chronically poor profitability, e.g. when suppliers or lenders readily finance entry but fixed financing costs trap the firm once in).
- Structural analysis should reduce -- not fuel -- debate over exact industry boundaries. If the five forces (including substitutes and potential entrants) are properly assessed, where the lines are drawn becomes largely irrelevant to strategy formulation.
- Defining an industry is not the same as defining where the firm wants to compete. A broadly defined industry does not mean the firm should or can compete broadly.
- Buyer selection is itself a strategic variable -- target buyer segments with less intrinsic bargaining power.
- Exit barriers and entry barriers are often related; high exit barriers with low entry barriers is the worst structural combination.

## Failure Modes

- **Treating all forces as equal.** The governing force (or forces) determines profitability; find it and weight the analysis toward it.
- **Ignoring government.** Government works through all five forces (as regulator of entry, as buyer/supplier, via subsidies affecting substitutes); it is "usually more illuminating to consider how government affects competition through the five competitive forces than to consider it as a force in and of itself." Omitting it leaves the analysis incomplete.
- **Confusing growth with attractiveness.** A fast-growing industry can have terrible structure (low barriers, commodity product, powerful buyers).
- **Confusing the short-run with the structural.** Business cycles, material shortages, and strikes are transient; structural analysis targets the permanent, underlying economic and technical characteristics of the industry. Fluctuating conditions have tactical, not structural, significance.
- **Static analysis only.** Porter insists on forecasting the magnitude of each underlying cause; assess trajectory, not just current state.
- **Too narrow on substitutes.** Substitutes can come from "businesses seemingly far removed from the industry" -- look broadly.
- **Listing without rating.** Each sub-factor must be assessed for strength, not merely named.
- **Endlessly debating industry boundaries instead of running the analysis.** The boundary question is a distraction once the five forces are assessed broadly.
