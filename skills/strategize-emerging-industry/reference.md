# Reference: strategize-emerging-industry

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- "Only in rare cases will it be feasible and profitable to defend a near monopoly market share as the industry grows rapidly."
- Early mobility barriers (proprietary technology, unique product) will erode. The firm "must be prepared to find new ways to defend its position."
- The nature of entrants shifts over time -- from small, newly created firms to established firms attracted by lower risk and competing on scale and marketing clout.
- Customers or suppliers may integrate into the industry as it grows.
- Conventional wisdom in emerging industries is often arbitrary, born from expedient early decisions, not analysis.
- **Shaping is the overriding strategic issue.** The firm should try to set the rules of the game -- product policy, marketing approach, pricing strategy -- within the constraints of underlying economics and its own resources, in a way that yields the strongest long-run position.
- **Balance industry advocacy against narrow self-interest.** Early on, the firm depends on the industry's shared credibility to induce buyer substitution -- promoting standardization, policing substandard quality, presenting a united front to suppliers/customers/government/lenders serves the firm too. But firms that take a high profile as industry spokespeople often fail to shift back toward their own position as the industry matures, and get left behind.
- **A firm may have to compete temporarily with a strategy it plans to abandon**, or participate in segments it intends to exit, simply to help develop the industry -- these "temporary" moves are a legitimate cost of shaping.
- **Pioneering is attractive when:** it builds a lasting learning curve advantage that later technological generations won't nullify, when customer loyalty compounds from being first, and when it locks in absolute cost advantages via early commitments to raw materials or distribution.
- **Pioneering is uniquely risky when:** the basis of early competition and segmentation will differ from what matters later (wrong skills built), when the costs of educating the market can't be made proprietary, when small early entrants will simply be replaced by more formidable late entrants, or when rapid technological change will obsolete early investment and hand later entrants a fresher-product advantage.
- **Defending high share is often partly an emotional reaction**, not a rational one -- pioneers frequently over-invest in responding to every competitive move instead of building their own strengths and developing the industry. It can even be appropriate to encourage certain competitors' entry (via licensing, etc.) because a known-quantity competitor is often preferable to inviting a major established firm in once the industry matures.

## Failure Modes

| Failure | Description |
|---|---|
| Defending monopoly share | Expending excessive resources to hold early share instead of building strengths and developing the industry |
| Relying on early barriers | Assuming proprietary technology or unique product variety will persist as barriers; failing to invest in scale/brand as barriers shift |
| Ignoring externalities | Pursuing narrow self-interest too early, degrading competitors when the industry needs a united front |
| Shifting too late | Continuing industry advocacy after penetration is established, failing to defend firm-specific position |
| Wrong timing bet | Pioneering when market segmentation basis will change, or waiting when learning curve advantages are decisive |
| Single-scenario planning | Committing to one forecast in a high-uncertainty environment instead of bounding outcomes with scenarios |
| Entering because of growth, not structure | Entering an emerging industry chiefly because it's growing fast or incumbents are currently profitable, rather than because structural analysis of the industry's eventual long-run configuration supports above-average returns |
