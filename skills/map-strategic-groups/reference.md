# Reference: map-strategic-groups

Consult during synthesis (heuristics) and before finalizing (failure modes).

## Heuristics

- The profit potential of a firm is a function of (a) the industry's common structural characteristics, (b) the structural characteristics of its strategic group (mobility barriers, bargaining power, substitute exposure, rivalry from other groups), and (c) the firm's position within its group.
- Structural strengths (mobility barriers, bargaining position) are relatively stable and difficult to overcome; implementational strengths (managerial ability, execution) are more ephemeral.
- When industry structure is unchanging, the cost of overcoming mobility barriers to join an occupied group may eliminate the benefits of doing so. Significant repositioning gains come when industry evolution lowers the cost of shifting.
- Sequenced entry lowers total cost and risk by segmenting the investment: enter via reversible investments first (e.g., private label production), then advance to groups requiring irreversible investments (advertising, R&D).
- **Mobility barriers are the internal analogue of entry barriers:** the same economic factors that keep outside firms out of an industry also raise the cost for an *existing* firm to shift its strategy and jump to a different group. This is why some strategic groups are persistently more profitable than others -- without mobility barriers, successful strategies would be imitated away.
- **Market interdependence is the single most important driver of inter-group rivalry** -- the degree to which different groups compete for the exact same customers. High interdependence (e.g., all groups selling to the same farmer in fertilizer) produces the most vigorous rivalry.
- **The size/equality rule:** the more numerous and equal in size the strategic groups, the more their strategic asymmetry increases rivalry volatility. Greatly unequal groups have little competitive impact on each other.
- **Strategic distance amplifies skirmishing:** the more strategies diverge between groups (brand identification, cost position, technological leadership, parent/government relationships), the more vigorous the competitive conflict, because firms with very different approaches struggle to understand each other's behavior and avoid mistaken reactions.
- **Axis selection:** the best strategic variables for map axes are those that determine the industry's key mobility barriers (e.g., brand identification and distribution channels in soft drinks). Avoid axes that move together -- choose variables that expose genuine diversity of strategic combinations.
- Axes need not be continuous or monotonic; discrete categories (e.g., channel mix) are valid and often the most illuminating choice.
- An industry can and should be mapped multiple times with different dimension pairs when a single map misses key competitive issues -- there is no single "right" map.
- Always include the firm's relationship to its parent company or host government as a mapping dimension -- it shapes objectives, resources, and often correlates with other strategic choices (e.g., all oil-company divisions in a fertilizer industry tend to share a similar strategy).
- **The "low cost is the only sustainable strategy" view is seriously misleading.** Achieving an overall low-cost position typically requires sacrificing the differentiation, technology, or service other groups' mobility barriers are built on -- it is not automatically dominant.
- When small-share specialist firms outperform broad-line leaders, resist the reflex to dismiss it as "just a narrow market definition." The real structural question is how the small firm built mobility barriers that shield it from the leader's scale economies.

## Failure Modes

1. **Choosing correlated dimensions** -- selecting axes that move together collapses the map into a diagonal line with no strategic insight.
2. **Making every firm its own group** -- over-segmenting destroys the analytical power of grouping; cluster firms with genuinely similar strategies.
3. **Ignoring mobility barriers** -- mapping groups without assessing what prevents movement between them misses the core structural insight.
4. **Using only one map** -- a single pair of axes may miss important strategic variation; consider multiple maps.
5. **Confusing quality with technological leadership** -- Porter explicitly warns these do not necessarily go together.
6. **Ignoring parent company and government relationships** -- these dimensions are easy to overlook but can be decisive in some industries.
7. **Assuming overall low-cost position is always the dominant strategy** -- ignores that low cost within a strategic group can be crucial while low cost overall is not necessarily achievable or even the right target.
